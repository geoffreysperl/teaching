## Effective urban educators are reflective practitioners who can accurately assess themselves and develop improvement plans.

When discussing being “reflective,” I take a different approach than many. During my time in IT, one of my responsibilities was continuous improvement. After a service was delivered to a client, my teams and I had to look at what could have been done better and how it could have been done better—on all parts. Being “reflective” isn’t solely about me as the teacher or my lesson. The teacher must use that reflection to understand the students’ needs, and the teacher needs to explain to the students to reflect upon their performance, too.

I focus on understanding how a lesson has gone, usually through observation, exit tickets, and my own notes. This allows me to review what went well (and what didn’t) when I look back on the lesson in the future.

For example, during the 2023-24 school year (my student teaching cycle), I taught a lesson on [Neil Gaiman's short story "Click-Clack the Rattlebag"](https://www.commonlit.org/en/texts/click-clack-the-rattle-bag) and compared it to [Edgar Allan Poe's "The Cask of Amontillado,"](https://poestories.com/read/amontillado). When I was planning, I decided to have the students handle the reading and response in a single class. Reflecting afterward, I thought I moved too quickly. Surprisingly, the reaction from the students overwhelmingly showed me that they loved the story, enjoyed the assignment, and didn’t seem pressed for time (I will still give more time when I teach it in the future).

During the 2022-23 school year, I was a long-term social studies substitute at Eastpointe Middle School (EMS) from mid-December to the end of school in mid-June. It was clear the 6th-grade students were tired of what they called “virtual” work (meaning anything assigned and completed online) after returning to full face-to-face instruction (before the second semester, EMS had been on a hybrid schedule). So, even though a computer-based assignment is much easier to grade, I moved all my assignments to be “real” (physical) to make it more engaging for them. This also had the added benefit of making it easier for the students without a working computer at home (or lacked Internet access). Yes, it made it more challenging to grade, but the students appreciated the tangibility of the physical work.

Further, as I worked with the students at EMS and reflected on their needs, I clearly needed to find ways to engage them on their ground. The earlier teacher had only finished the geography of the United States and Canada by mid-December, leaving me with the rest of North America and the other six continents. It was clear the topic wasn't engaging the students. Thus, I scrapped (with the principal's blessing) what the earlier teacher planned and created a new starting point—one-week unit focused on the cultural history of metro Detroit. Using that lesson, we then talked about how metro Detroit culturally connects to other areas we had left to cover (even Antarctica!), allowing us to discuss cultural geography (and, by extension, physical geography) that was relevant to them.

Finally, I knew I had to be creative with their assessments. I had seen them take a test with the earlier teacher, and they had taken a couple of quizzes with me, and they simply were not studying. We were finishing our unit on Africa at the end of the semester, and the second of Marvel's *Black Panther* movies had just recently come out, so I decided to have them explore how Wakanda (the fictional African country in *Black Panther*) draws from geography and culture across the entire continent. Keeping in mind that I was a substitute and a full-time graduate student, I found three TPT lessons that I heavily customized into a gallery walk as the assessment. I delivered a short lecture, had them watch a YouTube video on the connections between reality and the movies, and then had multiple reference packets across four media center stations. The results? I saw, by far, their best scores and highest completion rates from the two months I had been teaching them.

It was the time over these past two school years that taught me not only to be reflective about my teaching and assignments but also to listen to how the students are feeling and reacting. It might have meant a bit more work for me, but the students greatly appreciated not having to use their devices for a lot of the assignments I assigned. I think that, and my other examples, show that we need to listen and reflect to understand how we engage the students on their terms.
