Effective urban educators are Reflective Practitioners who can accurately assess themselves and develop plans for improvement. During my time in computing, one of my responsibilities was continuous improvement. After a service was delivered to a client, my teams and I had to look at what could have been done better and how it could have been done better. That same reflective practice follows me in all I do.

> For me, being "reflective" isn't solely about me as the teacher or my lesson. The teacher must use that reflection to understand the students' needs.

I focus on understanding how a lesson has gone, often asking students for exit tickets to gather their thoughts and quickly jotting mine down. This allows me to review what went well (and what didn't) when I look back on the lesson in the future.

During my student teaching cycle, when teaching a lesson on [Neil Gaiman's short story "Click-Clack the Rattlebag,"](https://www.commonlit.org/en/texts/click-clack-the-rattle-bag) and comparing it to [Edgar Allan Poe's "The Cask of Amontillado"](https://poestories.com/read/amontillado), I decided to have the students handle the reading and response in a single class. Reflecting afterwards, I thought I moved too quickly. Surprisingly, the response from the students overwhelmingly showed me that they loved the story, enjoyed the assignment, and didn't seem pressed for time (I will still give more time when I teach it in the future).

For me, being "reflective" isn't solely about ma as the teacher or my lesson. The teacher must use that reflection to understand the students' needs. When I was a long-term substitute at Eastpointe Middle School (EMS) last school year, it was clear the students were tiring of what they called "virtual" work (anything assigned and completed on the computer) after returning to full face-to-face instruction. So, even though a computer-based assignment is much easier to grade, I moved all my assignments to be "real" (physical) to make it more engaging for them. This also had the added benefit of making it easier for the students without a working computer at home (or lacked access to the Internet). It made it more difficult to grade, yes, but the students appreciated the tangibility of the physical work.

Further, as I worked with the 6th graders at EMS and reflected on their needs, it was clear I needed to find ways to engage them on their ground. I had already known a regular test wouldn't work as an assessment for them—they would get bored and ignore it. I needed an activity they could connect with. Thus, I scrapped what the earlier teacher planned and (due to a lack of time, as I was teaching full-time and taking nine credit hours as a graduate student) found three lessons on TPT that I could customize into a gallery walk. I had multiple packets (printed from slide decks) across four stations in the media center, and we finished our unit on Africa by exploring how Wakanda (from Marvel's *Black Panther* movies) draws from geography and culture across Africa. The results? I saw, by far, their best scores from the two months I had been teaching them.
