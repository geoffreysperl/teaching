## Effective urban educators are Reflective Practitioners who can accurately assess themselves and develop plans for improvement.

During my time in computing, one of my responsibilities was continuous improvement. After a service was delivered to a client, my teams and I had to look at what could have been done better and how it could have been done better. That same reflective practice follows me in all I do.

I focus on understanding how a lesson has gone, often asking students for exit tickets to gather their thoughts and quickly jotting mine down. This allows me to review what went well (and what didn't) when I look back on the lesson in the future. Recently, when teaching a lesson on [Neil Gaiman's short story "Click-Clack the Rattlebag,"](https://www.commonlit.org/en/texts/click-clack-the-rattle-bag) I decided to have the students handle the reading and response in a single class. While I thought I moved too quickly, the response from the students overwhelmingly showed me that they loved the story and enjoyed the assignment.

Being "reflective" isn't solely about the teacher or their lesson. The teacher must use that reflection to understand the students' needs. When I was a long-term substitute last school year, it was clear the students were tiring of what they called "virtual" work (anything assigned and completed on the computer) after returning to full face-to-face instruction. So, even though a computer-based assignment is much easier to grade, I moved all my assignments to be "real" (physical) to make it more engaging for them. This also had the added benefit of making it easier for the students without a working computer at home (or lacked access to the Internet).
